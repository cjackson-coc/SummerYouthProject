## Step 2 — What to present: tell a story, not a feature list

The mistake most people make is listing features: "there's a button, and a page, and a photo..."
Boring. Instead, tell the **story** of your site. Answer these — you don't need all of them, pick
what's true for you:

- **What is it?** ("It's my resume site / an about-me page / a page about the games I love.")
- **Who's it for?** ("Anyone who wants to know me — a teacher, a future job.")
- **What are you proud of?** The one thing you'd point at first.
- **What was hard?** The bug you beat, the thing that finally clicked.
- **What did you learn?** You started this summer knowing how much? Look how far that went.

Write your answers down in a few bullet points. That's your script — you don't memorize it, you just
know your beats.

---

## Step 3 — A structure you can lean on

Here's a simple order that works every time. Follow it and you'll never be lost:

1. **Who you are** — your name, one line about you.
2. **What you built** — show your live site on screen. "This is my site, it's live on the internet."
3. **Walk the front end** — click through it. Your resume, your second page, your dark mode button,
   your live-data feature. Let it *do* things while you talk.
4. **Go under the hood** — this is the part that makes people lean in. Open the **developer tools**
   (`F12`), use the hover-inspect tool, and show one piece of code you're proud of — your dark-mode
   toggle, your lightbox, a section you styled. "Here's the actual code behind this."
5. **What you learned** — a sentence or two. Honest beats impressive.
6. **Close** — "Thanks — I'm happy to answer questions."

> ⭐ **The front-end-then-back-end move is your secret weapon.** Anyone can show a website. Opening
> the dev tools and explaining the code behind it shows you actually *built* it and understand it.
> That's the moment people realize this is real.

## Step 4 — How to present well (the pro tips)

- **Have everything open and ready before you start.** Your live site in one tab, dev tools ready.
  Don't make people watch you find things. Fumbling kills confidence.
- **Practice out loud, at least twice.** Not in your head — out loud. It's always different when you
  actually say it. Time yourself.
- **Point at what you're talking about.** "This button here" while you hover it. It keeps people with
  you.
- **Slow down.** Everyone talks too fast when nervous. A deliberate pace sounds confident, and it
  buys you thinking time.
- **It's okay to be nervous.** Everyone is. Take a breath, and remember: you know this better than
  anyone watching.
- **Have an answer ready for one likely question.** "What was the hardest part?" or "What would you
  add next?" Think about your answer now so it doesn't catch you cold.

---

## Step 5 — Rehearse (do this twice)

**Run 1 — solo.** Open your site, start talking, go through your whole structure from Step 3 out
loud. Time it. Notice where you get stuck or ramble — those are the spots to tighten.

**Run 2 — to a person.** Grab a classmate and actually present to them. Have them tell you:
- One thing that was clear and good.
- One thing they'd tighten.

Then swap and do the same for theirs. Presenting to one friendly person is the best practice there
is for presenting to a room.
